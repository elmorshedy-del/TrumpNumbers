How this works, and what it can't tell you

What it does

It counts how many times Donald Trump posts on Truth Social, and projects where the current Monday-to-Sunday total will land. Roughly twenty statistical models compete on the same task, and every one of them is scored on forecasts of weeks it was never allowed to see.

Where the numbers come from

Posts are read from trumpstruth.org, a complete public mirror of the account. Truth Social's own API refuses requests from datacenter addresses, so the mirror is the practical ingest path; its robots.txt permits crawling. The archive runs from February 2022 to the present — over 34,000 posts.

Timestamps are stored in UTC and bucketed into US Eastern days, because his local day is the unit that produces the weekday pattern. In UTC, late-evening posts spill into the next day and smear it.

ReTruths are excluded from the modelled series. The mirror records a ReTruth under the original author's timestamp, not the moment Trump reshared it — one ReTruth of a month-old post is stored with the month-old date. Counting them would credit activity to days on which he did nothing. They are still ingested, labelled, and shown in the live feed.

How the models are graded

Every model outputs a full distribution, never a single number, and is scored with proper scoring rules — rules under which the best possible score comes from reporting what you actually believe. This is not a stylistic preference. Grade forecasts the wrong way and you do not merely measure badly; you teach the forecaster to lie. A desert forecaster who says "0% rain" every day is right 98% of the time and has told you nothing.

The primary metric is CRPS, in units of posts. Alongside it sit the log score, pinball loss, Brier scores for threshold events, and a PIT-based calibration test. Accuracy is deliberately absent: a model can be more accurate on average while being systematically overconfident, and overconfidence is the failure that costs you, because it stays invisible until the tail event arrives.

Validation is walk-forward: train on the past, forecast the next week, roll forward. A random train/test split would let a model see Tuesday and Thursday while being scored on Wednesday — not mild optimism, but reading tomorrow's newspaper. A one-day embargo sits at the seam, and a final stretch of weeks is held back untouched.

What the data turned out to be

  • Not Poisson, by a wide margin. Variance runs many times the mean. A Poisson process at this rate would essentially never produce the days actually observed.
  • Heavy-tailed. The busiest day is many times the median, so every figure here is a median with an asymmetric interval rather than a mean plus or minus something.
  • Almost no day-to-day memory. Once the weekly cycle is removed, autocorrelation is close to nothing. Whatever makes posts cluster operates within a day, not across days.
  • Weeks are hard to forecast. The best model removes only a small share of climatology's error. That is the honest headline, and it is why the leaderboard shows skill scores instead of raw numbers that could be dressed up.

What it cannot tell you

It cannot tell you why he posted a lot on a given day. Two stories explain clustering equally well: something outside caused it (a storm knocks down forty trees at once), or the events cause each other (an earthquake, then aftershocks, each with aftershocks of their own). These produce remarkably similar daily counts, and separating them is a live research problem, not a textbook exercise. Both are fitted; neither is asserted. The burst keywords on the diagnostics page describe what was in a heavy day — they do not explain it.

Tail probabilities are the softest numbers here and are marked as such. Rare events need far more data to pin down than the middle of a distribution does, and roughly fifty scored weeks is a coarse instrument.

Running it

The project is a Python pipeline plus this static site. python daemon.py polls for new posts, recomputes the projection, re-runs the backtest nightly, and serves these pages locally. See the README for one-off commands and for running it unattended.

This is a descriptive forecasting exercise about public posting volume. It makes no claims about anyone's intentions and does not attribute causes to bursts.
